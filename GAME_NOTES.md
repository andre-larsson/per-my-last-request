## Four assignments (added 2026-11-14, provisional until playtested)

User request: "add some more levels … the jump from tutorial level to final one is too much added at once." The previous two-assignment version is commit `5be3f6d` plus the uncommitted pace work.

- **01 First instructions → 02 Fair warning → 03 Prize money → 04 The full circuit.** The two new ones split what the circuit used to add all at once. Each is a course through some of the circuit's own stops, in circuit order, with the same opponents, prices and rules, so nothing learned is unlearned later.
  - **Fair warning** (`warning`): camp, rat, boar, Quickfoot, home; 3 stamps; 16 turns. New: announced moves, guard and its counter, armor, agility, winded.
  - **Prize money** (`prize`): the circuit's second half, from the purse to the home stretch (purse, prize bruiser, medic, Quickfoot, armorer, bench, champion); Quickfoot's stamp and the champion; 30 turns (first set at 24, then 28; see the model runs below). New: coins, the optional prize fight, medic, armorer, bench, bracing, the champion, and a clock that starts to matter.
  - **The full circuit** then adds only the combination: both halves in one trial, 32 turns for 12 stops, commendations.
- **First drafts teach the new rule.** In both middle assignments, "An unfortunate first draft" is the previous assignment's careful orders, unchanged. The tutorial's orders ("fight … using quick strikes") die at Quickfoot after taking the boar's charges. Fair warning's orders ("Quick strike every turn, except: guard against a SMASH.") die at the champion with both potions unused; since 2026-11-24 Prize money's first draft adds "Buy nothing.", because the 3B otherwise bought the sword unasked and won (see "Example orders, the prize fight, and what sways the 3B"). The careful orders for Prize money fight the bruiser, buy the sword, guard smashes and drink at LOW, and say to skip the bench and submit as soon as possible (scripted: won on turn 22 of 30).
- **Rules live in one table**, `LEVELS` in `src/game.js`: course, turn limit, stamps, champion, announced moves, commendations, example orders, objective text. The tutorial and the circuit are unchanged: every prompt, action list and resulting state over 16,367 states of tutorial and circuit play hashes the same as before.
- **Maps**: the tutorial and the circuit keep their hand-placed layouts. The middle assignments space their stops evenly along the same loop (`spreadLayout` in `src/map.js`). On a phone, Prize money's loop is taller and camp sits a little above the middle so the first labels do not collide.
- **Tabs**: four in one row when the trial column is wide enough, otherwise two by two (a container query on the tab row).
- **Commendations** stay in the full circuit only.

Proxies (`node tools/balance.mjs --level …`, the same 540 scripted order styles):

| | Fair warning | Prize money | Full circuit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Styles that win | 89% | 92% | 56% |
| Quick strikes that ignore the enemy | 0 of 60 | 29 of 60 | see above |
| Median winning trial | 14 of 16 turns | 22 of 30 | 29 of 32 |
| Turns per stop | 3.2 | 3.3 | 2.7 |

The style grid does not rank the two middle assignments: both are forgiving for scripted bots, which waste no turns. What makes Prize money harder is what the orders must cover (potions, the side bet, shopping, the champion's routine) and the champion: 42 of its 44 losing styles die there. The clock binds the real model, which wastes turns the bots do not (see the model runs).

Known soft spot: Fair warning is also won by heavy strikes that ignore the announcements (the boar dies in two heavy strikes). Only quick strikes that ignore the SMASH lose. It is a gentle step by design.

**Real model (Qwen 2.5 3B, RTX 4090, normal pace), `npm run test:llm -- --levels`:**

- **Fair warning, 16 turns.** Careful orders: **won on turn 15** with 33 HP, both charges guarded, every ordinary hit struck. It drank a potion after Quickfoot at 15 HP, which the orders never mention; that used the one spare turn. First draft: **lost**. It took all three charges unguarded and drank both potions to survive, then ran out of turns on the home stretch (scripted, it dies at Quickfoot instead).
- **Prize money, how the limit and the careful orders were chosen.** At 24 turns, the first careful orders ("Fight the prize bruiser and buy a sharper sword with the winnings. Quick strike every turn, except: guard against a SMASH and drink a potion when health is LOW.") beat the champion on turn 24 and lost on time. Unordered extras each cost a turn: a potion at 29 HP, a second purchase (a potion), a rest on the bench. At 28 turns, the same orders reached camp and then drank a potion at 34 HP instead of submitting. Three wordings at 28 turns: adding "Do not rest or buy anything else" made the model guard 8 of 9 ordinary hits and lose on time. The circuit-style "retreat from the optional prize fight" made it retreat from the champion as well. "…Fight the prize bruiser, buy only the sharper sword, skip the bench, and submit at camp as soon as you can." won on turn 27. That is the careful example now, and the limit is 30.
- **Prize money, 30 turns.** Careful orders: **won on turn 27** with 20 HP, all 3 smashes guarded, but 2 of 5 ordinary hits guarded, a potion drunk at 30 HP and an extra potion bought. First draft: **lost on time**. It guarded 7 of 9 ordinary hits and was on the home stretch at turn 30, with the champion beaten (scripted, it dies at the champion instead).
- What this model does: it guards ordinary hits, drinks and shops unasked, and changes behaviour with small changes of wording. The middle assignments' clocks leave room for that. Both first drafts still fail with the model, but by the clock, not the way the scripted check predicts. (With the prompts of 2026-11-24, the paragraph and then `focus`, Prize money's first draft won both of its interface runs: see "Prompt tuning on the 3B". It now ends "Buy nothing.": see "Example orders, the prize fight, and what sways the 3B".) Each result is one greedy run on one GPU.

**Known prompt error, not fixed:** the move line for a WINDED opponent says "your strikes deal double" (`moveFact` in `src/protocol.js`), but the engine has never doubled them; the action lines show the true damage. It is left from the stagger design. Fixing it changes the circuit prompt that the probe and the circuit run were measured with, so it needs those re-run.

## Prose challenges for the model: tested, not built (2026-11-23)

User question: can stops be redesigned so that the small local model wins where a rule-based or a random hero does not? Six ideas put the deciding fact in prose instead of a label: the opponent's move told as a sentence ("tells"); a noticeboard memo that amends the orders; armorer stock that is described, not named; an inspector or an impostor on the home stretch; an opponent described by traits; a deal offered by the bruiser. They were tested with real models before anything was built. **Result: asked as the game asks today, no model wins, from 3B to 8B. Keyword rules beat all six models on total decisions. The 3B models do about as well as a hero that does not read; the 4B, 7B and 8B do somewhat better (65–74% against 58%) and stay well below the rules (89%). With a different setup (the model first answers a question about the scene, then decides on a labelled situation), Qwen 3 8B draws level with the rules and Qwen 2.5 7B comes close on the count, on a comparison that is no longer like for like: see "Tuning" below. The game is unchanged.**

- **Probe** (`node tools/ideas/probe.mjs`): 118 single decisions with one right answer, each asked under two versions of the standing orders. 47 use development wordings and 71 are held out. Contestants: the model, asked as the game asks it (same system prompt, one action ID as JSON, temperature 0); keyword rules (`tools/ideas/keyword-bot.mjs`, 616 lines, written by a separate agent that saw only the development wordings); the best fixed answer per stop, which is what a hero can do without reading; a random pick.

- **The 3B can read one plain question; it cannot then follow the orders.** Asked a neutral yes/no question about the scene ("Is the enemy winding up for its big attack this turn?", `IDEAS_NEUTRAL=1`), Qwen 2.5 3B was right on 80% of held-out tells and on 9 of 9 held-out inspector scenes, as good as the keyword rules or better (80% and 67%). The first wording of those two questions added "Answer no for…", and the model then answered "no" 48 times of 48; the `read` row above has that fault for tells and inspector. With the answer written into the checklist, the decision was still often wrong: 83% on tells, 88–89% on a two-way choice (opponent, offer), 47% on the notice ("do X, unless the notice says otherwise"), and 22% at the inspector, where it chose "pay" in 22 of 28 decisions against "Never pay". With the pay option removed (`IDEAS_NO_PAY=1`) it refused nearly everyone, seal or no seal (72%). The best 3B result against the rules was a tie: 80% on tells, neutral question first, one version of the orders (65% with the other).
- **Wording of the orders moved results a lot**: the opponent idea scored 58% and 92% for two versions of the same orders (all 12 cases, plain prompt).
- **Whole trials** (`node tools/ideas/trial.mjs --level prize --seeds 12`): Prize money on the real engine, with each opponent's move drawn at random every turn from its routine. With today's labels, Qwen 2.5 3B **won 12 of 12** and guarded 25 of 29 smashes. With the move told in prose and no label, it won 9 of 12 and guarded 3 of 27 big attacks. Scripted heroes won 12 of 12, with or without reading.
- **Reading is not needed to win on today's balance.** Over 600 seeded trials with drawn moves, the best scripted hero that ignores the opponent's move wins 100% of Fair warning, Prize money and the full circuit, and 96% of a boar, bruiser and champion course. No hero can win because it reads until an unguarded smash costs the trial.
- **What holds up.** Drawn moves with labels: the 3B needs no fixed cycle (12 of 12). The labels and the checklist: they are what a 3B model acts on. A model-made checklist (one neutral yes/no question per label) is the only form in which prose came close.
- **Limits.** One author wrote every wording. 71 held-out cases, one run per model, temperature 0. The keyword rules are rule-based when they run, but a large model spent about 35 minutes writing them. Qwen 3 4B is one plain run. The 7B and 8B rows above are one plain run each; other ways of asking them are under "Tuning" below, and no whole trials were played with them. The 7B and 8B do not fit the 3060 Ti's 8 GB beside the desktop and ran on the RTX 4090. The 4B's float16 build failed to load on the 3060 Ti ("Invalid ShaderModule"); the float32 build loaded.
- **Larger models, run 2026-11-23 on the RTX 4090** (`QUEST_MODEL=Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-q4f32_1-MLC node tools/ideas/probe.mjs`, then `QUEST_MODEL=Qwen3-8B-q4f32_1-MLC`; plain prompt; both got the 9 labelled control decisions right). Qwen 2.5 7B got 99 of 142 held-out decisions right and Qwen 3 8B 95, against 125 for the keyword rules. Neither clears the rules' row, and neither matches the 4B's single run. The rules were right where the model was wrong 32 times (7B) and 42 times (8B); the reverse happened 6 and 12 times. The one idea with a model ahead is the inspector, 7B 13 of 18 against the rules' 12, and it got there by refusing 15 times of 18, which is the no-reading answer (12 of 18). The 8B chose "show" 16 times of 18 there. Both met the held-out opponents with heavy 31 times of 32. On tells the 7B guarded 4 of 14 big attacks (none under the second version of the orders, where it chose quick 20 times of 20) and the 8B guarded 7 of 14. No idea cleared the bar set for building one, so no whole trials were played with these models.
- **Tuning the prompt and the setup (2026-11-23, RTX 4090; `node tools/ideas/tune.mjs`).** User question: can the models be brought closer to the rules? Every variant was tuned on the 47 development wordings only; the held-out wordings were run three times in all (once for the 7B, twice for the 8B), scores only. The facts and the offered actions stayed as they were, so the rules' answers did not change.

  - **What worked: two steps.** First the model answers the game's own question about the scene, one fixed question per kind of stop ("Is the enemy starting its big attack this turn? Answer no for…"). Then the game writes the answer in place of the prose ("Enemy's move this turn: BIG ATTACK…", "This person does NOT show the guild's red wax seal") and the model decides on that labelled situation, as it does in the game today. On the development wordings this took the 7B from 68 to 81 of 94 and the 8B from 72 to 83.
  - **Reading was not the main weak part; deciding was.** Asked one focused question, the 7B read 68 of 73 development scenes correctly and the 8B 66. With the true answers added as a checklist under the prose, the 7B still got only 69 of 94 decisions and the 8B 79: the prose kept pulling against the checklist line. With the true answers in place of the prose: 85 and 88. Repeating the orders after the offered actions helped the 8B again (89) and not the 7B (83). The notice is the exception: with the true answers written in, the 8B still gets 27 of 32 held-out notice decisions.
  - **What did not work** (development wordings, of 94; plain is 68 for the 7B and 72 for the 8B): one sentence of thought before the action (7B 60, its thoughts drift into tactics: "guard to block the damage"); naming what is seen and quoting the order (64); stating the question the orders pose (7B 68, 8B 54); telling the model it is a clerk applying orders, not a player (66, 70); the orders repeated last, on prose (67, 72); Qwen 3's own thinking mode (70, at 9 s a decision, with 9 replies that gave no usable answer within 900 tokens); one yes/no question per offered action, with true labels (72, 83).
  - **The question wording is per model.** The guided tell question ("Answer no for… a big attack that it starts and then abandons") is right 14 of 14 for the 7B, makes the 8B say no to real wind-ups (11 of 14), and made the 3B say no every time. The 8B does better with two plain questions, "does it begin to wind up" and "does it break off" (13 of 14, held-out 19 of 20). A choice between four kinds, made-up examples in the question, and a sentence of description before the answer did no better than these.
  - **How far to trust it: level with the rules, not ahead.** 129 against 125 is four decisions: the 8B was right where the rules were wrong 15 times, and the reverse 11 times (exact McNemar p = 0.56). The two versions of the orders share one reading, so the 142 decisions are 71 scenes: the 8B does better than the rules on 9 scenes and worse on 9, and each gets both orders right on 61. The 7B is better on 9 and worse on 15. It is the 8B's second held-out run; the change between the two was picked on development scores, but after the first held-out score (117) was seen, and only the tells moved.
  - **Where the gains are.** The two ideas that turn on a trick: tells (38 of 40 for both models, rules 32) and the inspector (16 of 18, rules 12). The rules stay ahead on the notice (31 of 32 against 27). With the true answers written in, the 8B gets 137 of 142 and the 7B 129, so the 8B's remaining errors are mostly misreadings (9 of 13) and the 7B's mostly decisions (11 of 19).
  - **The comparison is not like for like.** The rules still read the raw prose. The model gets a question that the game wrote for that kind of stop, and then a label in place of the prose. Several labels use the words of the first version of the orders ("winding up for its big attack", "armored and slow", "makes strikes hit harder"), and it shows: with true labels, the 7B gets 8 of 8 held-out opponents under those orders and chooses heavy for all 8 under the second version. The guided inspector question ("Answer no for a seal that is drawn, promised or the wrong colour…") was written with the held-out scenes by the same author and names traps that occur in them; the inspector gain over the rules is on genuine inspectors that the rules refuse, not on the traps.
  - **The model reads; a fixed rule could decide.** Taking the model's recorded readings and applying the two test orders as fixed rules gives 134 of 142 (8B) and 135 (7B), against 129 and 123 when the model also decides, and 125 for the keyword rules on prose. So the model's reading is what beats the rules, and its deciding call loses 5 to 12 decisions. A fixed rule exists only because these two orders are known in advance; the game's orders are free text.
  - **Once the scene is labelled, the wrong decisions are systematic** (worked out from the true-label records; no new runs). Grouped by idea, orders, class and wanted action, the held-out decisions are 57 kinds of situation. The 7B is wrong every time in 6 of them, and those hold 11 of its 13 errors (the "fast, slippery" opponent met with heavy 4 times of 4). The 8B: 4 kinds, 4 of its 5 errors. The 3B, development wordings: 13 of 56 kinds, 18 of 19 errors. Many kinds have a single scene. So one labelled situation of each kind, asked at the desk with the player's orders, would show most of these errors before a trial. It would not show a misread scene.
  - **Cost.** Two to four model calls per decision (the armorer asks one question per item): about 1.9 s (7B) and 3.3 s (8B) on the RTX 4090, against 0.9 s plain. The game must write one question and one label per kind of stop; the model no longer sees the prose when it decides.
  - **The default 3B does not get there.** Development wordings only: plain 47 of 94; read then decide 65 (the neutral question, orders repeated last; 54 with the guided question); 75 with the true answers written in. It answers fewer reading questions correctly (62 of 73) and decides worse.
  - **Not tried:** Qwen 3 4B with this setup; whole trials (`tools/ideas/trial.mjs` asks the plain way); the two-part tell question on the 7B; orders that differ more from the labels. **A fair next test** needs fresh wordings from another author, variants frozen beforehand, and the fixed-rule replay as a control. The `shots` questions must stay off the held-out wordings: one made-up example (a seal in green wax) turned out to match a held-out scene, so `tune.mjs` refuses that pair.
- **Options to make the orders worth more, none built (user question, 2026-11-23).** Options 1 and 2 were then tried on the 3B: see "Rehearsal and orders last" below. In the order first suggested:
  1. A rehearsal at the desk: the model answers a dozen labelled situations with the player's orders, and the player sees the answers before the trial (the open "read the orders back" idea).
  2. The orders repeated after the offered actions, for the default 3B: 65 to 75 of 94 with true labels on the development wordings. The game's prompt has the orders in the system message only. It changes the pinned tutorial prompt, so that needs verifying again.
  3. Build only the two ideas where the model's reading beat the rules (tells with feints, the inspector), and make a wrong reading there cost the trial.
  4. Let the brief vary per trial (which seal is genuine, which tell is the big attack), so the orders carry what a rule written beforehand cannot know.
  5. The model reads the scene and a table made once from the orders decides. This needs the user's decision: it moves the per-turn choice out of the model.
  6. Measure with whole trials over many differently worded orders, not single decisions under two orders.

## Rehearsal and orders last, tried on the 3B (2026-11-23)

User request: "try the rehearsal and the orders-last test on the 3B". Both use the game's own labelled prompt on the real engine (`node tools/ideas/rehearse.mjs`). **Result: repeating the orders after the actions does not help. A rehearsal shows most of what the hero will do and catches orders it misreads wholesale, but it is too coarse to tune orders that already work. Nothing in the game changed.**

- **Setup.** Nine plans, three for each later assignment, written as data: what the player means in every state. A script that follows a plan wins the game as it is (Fair warning on turn 12 to 15 of 16, Prize money 22 to 25 of 30, the full circuit 30 to 31 of 32). Each plan is written as four orders, fixed before any model run: the game's example style ("Quick strike every turn, except: …"), a numbered list, plain speech, and words the labels do not use ("block", "big hit", "badly hurt"). That is 36 orders. Qwen 2.5 3B, temperature 0, RTX 4090; the harness gets the same 11 of 13 on the probe situations as `tools/probe.mjs`.
  - A **rehearsal** asks the model one decision for each of 16 (Fair warning), 24 (Prize money) or 30 (full circuit) situations: every kind of decision a script meets on that assignment, plus LOW health. Grouped as a short list they are 10, 18 and 17 kinds.
  - Each orders then plays four **trials**: the game as it is, and three with drawn moves. A decision is "as intended" when the plan allows it. Whatever the orders do not mention (medic, bench, a potion when not LOW) counts as a departure.
  - Both prompts did all of it: 7,691 decisions in 78 minutes.
- **Orders last: no gain.** The orders were repeated after the offered actions, worded as in the prose experiment. Probe: 11 of 13 either way. The 840 rehearsal situations: 505 as intended against 507 (57 better one way, 59 the other). Trial decisions as intended: 42% shipped, 39% with the orders last. Trials won: 40 against 45 of 144 (2 won only with the shipped prompt, 7 only with orders last; exact p = 0.18). With the orders last the hero drank more potions on the road when not LOW (53 times against 13, in the two wordings that work). The prompt stays as it is.
- **The wording decides most.** Trials won with the shipped prompt: example style 21 of 36, plain speech 19, numbered list 0, other words 0. With the list and with other words the hero guards: 1,462 of 1,617 fight decisions, and 48 of 72 trials never got past the practice rat. In the rewriting test below, one list written on one line ("Rule 1: quick strike every turn. Rule 2: guard against a SMASH, and only then.") works; three with the rules on numbered lines do not, whichever rule comes first.
- **By assignment**, the two wordings that work: Fair warning 21 of 24 trials, Prize money 18 of 24, the full circuit 1 of 24. Of those 24 circuit trials, 10 died at the boar after striking into its charge, 2 died at the bruiser, 11 ran out of turns and 1 won. The clock is lost to the known habits: it guards the champion's ordinary strike, visits the medic, rests on the bench and drinks on the road, and the plans have one or two turns to spare. It also does not drink at LOW in a fight.
- **What a rehearsal shows** (shipped prompt):
  - The very same prompt got the same answer in rehearsal and trial, 317 times of 317.
  - 93% of trial decisions match the rehearsal answer for their kind (2,611 of 2,801); with the short list 90%. Only 22 of 2,999 decisions were of a kind in neither list.
  - Where the rehearsal answer was not what the plan intends, the trials departed in 92% of those decisions (1,629 of 1,773); where it was as intended, in 8% (94 of 1,204). So 94% of all departures were in a kind the short list had flagged.
  - A hero that only looks decisions up in the rehearsal, played on the engine with no model, forecasts won or lost correctly for 135 of 144 trials (104 were lost; it calls 5 losses wins and 4 wins losses).
  - Choosing, per plan, the wording with the cleanest rehearsal wins 49% of trials; a wording at random 28%; the best one in hindsight 64%. The third of the orders with the worst rehearsals won 1 trial of 48.
- **What it does not show.**
  - Between the two wordings that work, the cleaner rehearsal did not pick the one that won more: with the shipped prompt it belonged to the worse wording in all 3 plans where they differ (6 no difference); with the orders last, to the better one in 3 and the worse in 2.
  - The answer moves with the numbers. With the careful circuit orders the hero guards the boar's charge at 40 HP and no stamps (the probe's situation) and strikes into it at 37 HP with one stamp (the rehearsal's).
  - **Rewriting by rehearsal** (the same author as the player; each rewrite judged by its rehearsal only, then four trials): five orders, 18 rehearsals. Every rehearsal improved (17 to 21 of 30, 13 to 18 of 24, 9 to 13 of 16, 16 to 20 of 30, 16 to 23 of 30). Trials won went from 4 of 20 to 5 of 20. The numbered list for Fair warning was repaired (0 to 4 of 4). The frugal Prize money orders got worse (3 to 0 of 4): the hero now retreated from Quickfoot, which the rehearsal had answered with a quick strike. The three circuit orders still lost, mostly on time. Several rewrites made the rehearsal worse (an added "never guard an ordinary strike" made the hero retreat from the champion), and the rehearsal showed that in seconds.
- **What follows.** A rehearsal is worth building as a quick check that the hero has understood the orders at all. All 18 list and other-words orders showed a guard against an ordinary strike in rehearsal, and all 72 of their trials were lost. At the 0.6 seconds a decision measured in this run, the short list takes 6 to 11 seconds and the long one 10 to 18. It should not be shown as a forecast. With this model the limit on the circuit is its habits, not the wording. A silent run of the whole trial costs as many decisions as the long list and is exact at temperature 0, but it gives the result away.
- **Limits.** One author wrote the plans, the wordings and the rewrites. One model, temperature 0, one GPU. The situations come from the same scripts that define the plans. Strict reading of the plan.

## Read-back (added 2026-11-24, provisional until playtested)

User goal: make writing orders pay off better than rule-based or random play, through the context the model gets, the tasks, or a mechanic that makes writing orders more rewarding. The rehearsal above was the best-supported option, so it is now in the game.

- **Only what a trial has met** (user feedback the same day: a full read-back takes the surprise out of each loop and makes trial and error mechanical). The game records, per assignment and in this browser (`pmlq.met.<level>`), the drill kinds its trials have met (`kindOf` in `src/drill.js`; forced moves do not count), and a read-back asks only about those. Before the first trial of an assignment there is nothing to read back ("Run a trial first"); after a trial that met something new, the button says so. Stops and moves not yet seen stay unseen, and unforeseen events (below, if built) are never in it.
- **What the player sees.** Under the letter, in Local LLM mode with a model loaded: "Ask the hero to read them back". The hero answers one decision for each situation met so far and the answers fill a reply memo line by line ("Re: your orders. Per your last request, this is what I would do: Tin boar is about to SMASH → Guard …"). No turn is spent and the trial is untouched. After the orders change, the memo dims and says so; the next read-back marks each answer that changed ("CHANGED"), so an edit shows its effect at once. At temperature 0 the button rests ("Read back ✓") until the orders, model or temperature change.
- **The drill** (`src/drill.js`): real engine states, found by walking the course once with a fixed route (quick strikes, guard a SMASH, a potion at LOW, the prize fight, the medic, the sword). The route only finds states and never plays the hero. One state per kind: ordinary strike, SMASH, BRACING, WINDED, LOW health in a fight, the optional prize fight, medic, armorer, bench, road, camp ready to submit. LOW health appears only in its own line; elsewhere the hero is hurt (28 HP) with a potion, so that unasked potions, medic visits and rests show. Tutorial 4 lines, Fair warning 6, Prize money 11, the circuit 11. The prompt is the game's own for that state (`decisionMessages`), so the memo shows what the hero would do there in a trial.
- **Why.** In the 3B run above, 93% of trial decisions matched the rehearsal answer for their kind, 94% of departures from the orders were in a kind the short list had flagged. One line alone sorts orders well: the 29 orders whose hero guarded an ordinary strike in rehearsal won 15 of their 116 trials; the other 7 won 25 of 28. It is shown as the hero's understanding, not as a forecast: the memo says that the numbers differ in a trial.
- **Cost.** One decision per line, through the same worker queue as a trial: about 0.6 s a line for the 3B on the RTX 4090 (7 s for the circuit), more on smaller GPUs. Starting a run, changing the assignment, the controller or the model stops it; a model error is shown in the memo and spends no turn.
- **Tests.** `tests/drill.test.mjs` (the drill, its states, the kinds a trial records, the memo markup); `npm run test:browser` with the stub worker: no read-back before a trial, then one trial, then a read-back of the three situations it met (one request each, the typed orders, no turn spent), stale after an edit, the changed answer marked.

## Unforeseen incidents: tested, not built (2026-11-24)

User feedback on the read-back: it may take the surprise out of each loop; the game may need random elements that cannot be known beforehand, where a model reading the orders could respond better than rules written in advance. Tested with the real models first (`node tools/ideas/incidents.mjs`). **Result: neither model applies a general stance to an unforeseen offer well enough. The 3B is at chance; the 8B takes nearly every offer, so it does no better than a hero that knows the kinds of incident without reading them. Nothing was built.**

- **The incidents.** Eight kinds of offer met on the road, each with random terms, so a stance gives different answers within one kind: a tinker who hones the blade, an herbalist, a cart (fast and maybe rough, or slow and restful), a petition (pays, is free, or has a fee), a bookmaker, a potion in a ditch, a toll (pay, or the long way round or over the fence), a shortcut or a scenic path. Each is told in prose; each option's exact terms are in its action line, as at the armorer ("Costs 3 of 6 coins. Takes one turn."), and walking on costs nothing.
- **The orders** are a plan's example orders plus one general stance, in two wordings each: take an offer if it costs no turn (or no coins, or no health), and walk past it if it does. Whether a choice follows the stance is decided by the option's terms. 64 incidents at real road states from the three later assignments, each asked under the six stance sentences and with none: 448 decisions per model, temperature 0, the game's own prompt with one line added ("SOMETHING UNEXPECTED: …").
- **Bars.** Rules written before the incidents are known can only always take the offer (236 of 384, 61%), always walk on (162, 42%) or pick at random (200, 52%). A hero that knows each kind of incident but not its terms, with the best fixed answer per stance and kind, gets 300 (78%).

  | Stance followed, of 384 | All | Stance says take it (208) | Stance says walk on (128) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Qwen 2.5 3B | 188 (49%) | 75 | 80 |
  | Qwen 2.5 3B, terms also stated as yes/no lines ("takes a turn: yes; costs coins: no; …") | 171 (45%) | 73 | 65 |
  | Qwen 3 8B | 271 (71%) | 204 | 29 |
  | Qwen 3 8B, terms as yes/no lines | 256 (67%) | 205 | 9 |

  (The other 48 decisions are tolls, where both options are offers.)
- **What fails is refusing.** The 8B took an offer in 351 of 384 decisions and, with no stance in the orders, 64 times of 64. It refused a honed blade, a paid ride or a bet because of a cost the orders named in 29 of 128 cases. The 3B's answers barely depend on the stance. Labelling the terms as yes/no, which is what made the fight checklist work, helped neither model: the 8B then took the offer 372 times of 384.
- **What it means for the design.** Surprise as such is easy to add (random terms, random kinds, drawn opponent moves). Surprise where the model's reading of free orders beats a rule written beforehand is not there with these models: the 8B's 71% is a habit of accepting, not reading. Rules written with the kinds in hand would do better (78%).
- **A surprise the models do handle: drawn opponent moves** (from the rehearsal records above, no new runs). Each opponent's moves drawn at random per trial from its own routine, still announced and labelled. Trials won, game as it is against drawn moves: 3B Fair warning 5 of 12 against 16 of 36 (42% and 44%), Prize money 4 of 12 against 14 of 36 (33%, 39%), circuit 1 of 12 against 0 of 36; 8B Fair warning 75% against 69%, Prize money 100% against 78%, circuit 67% against 36%. The plans' scripts win every drawn Fair warning and Prize money trial and 7 of 9 drawn circuits. So a per-trial draw keeps each loop different without asking for more reading than the checklist gives; it rewards orders that read the announced move over orders that rely on the order of moves, but a rule keyed to the checklist would do as well as the model.
- **Not tried:** stances phrased as refusals only ("never pay for anything unplanned"); a first step that asks the model which stance applies, as in the prose experiment's read-then-decide; whole trials with incidents; other models.
- Records: `artifacts/ideas-incidents-<model>[-labels].json`; logs `artifacts/tune-logs/incidents-*.txt`.

## Battle balance (reworked 2026-11-14)

The previous balance is tagged `balance-before`. The user chose all four changes: enemy intents with Guard, an armorer choice with a paying side bet, commendations, and a tighter clock, keeping overall difficulty about the same. **The full circuit only.** The introductory assignment is unchanged; `tests/game.test.mjs` pins its exact prompt text, because it is the verified model demo.

- **Announced moves.** Each circuit opponent repeats a fixed routine and announces its move before the hero acts: *strike* (ordinary hit), *smash* (big hit), *brace* (no hit, strikes deal half), *winded* (no hit, agile cannot dodge). The prompt uses the same capitalised words (SMASH, BRACING, WINDED, LOW) so orders can match them directly.
- **Guard** (from Fair warning on; the circuit originally): deal nothing, block 8 (14 with buckler), +1 stamina. **Guarding a smash counters automatically** for a heavy strike's damage. The first design (guarding a smash staggers; the *next* strike deals double) worked for bots, but Qwen 3B could not follow a two-step rule like "after a stagger, use heavy". The counter keeps the payoff in one decision.
- **Checklist.** Circuit prompts end with yes/no lines (Enemy about to SMASH, Enemy BRACING, Enemy WINDED, Health LOW, Optional prize fight), in and out of fights. The probe went from 6 to 8 of 10 with it at the time; it also stopped the model drinking potions on the road at high health.
- Routines: rat Nibble 3; boar Charge 12 → Gore 4; bruiser (20 HP, pays 6) Jab 5 → Haymaker 14 → Showboat; Quickfoot Dart 5 → Dart 5 → Out of breath; champion (**30 HP**) Jab 7 → Raise shield → Overhead smash 18 → Catch breath. Laps add 1 damage per move.
- **Armorer**: sword (+2) or buckler for 8 coins, potion for 6, each a turn. Without the bruiser you can afford one item, and with it two.
- **Bench** now also heals 6 (was stamina only; only 17 of 254 winners used it). **Turn limit 38 → 32.**
- **Old values, for reverting:** limit 38, sword 6 coins, bruiser 14 HP and 2 coins, bench stamina only, champion 32 HP hitting 9 every turn. During this rework the champion was 32 HP with the routine; it went to 30 because two real-model runs died to it about 2 HP short (bots: 54% → 56% of styles win).
- **Commendations** (optional, shown in the verdict): Ahead of schedule (by turn 26), No expense claims (no potion drunk), Overtime (beat the bruiser), By the book (no unguarded smash). They pull in different directions; 3 of 301 winning styles earn all four.
- **Example orders** are one sentence: careful "Quick strike every turn, except: guard against a SMASH, drink a potion when health is LOW, and retreat from the optional prize fight." A heavy-based example ("Use heavy strikes, but guard…") was tried and dropped: the 3B then skipped guards and retreated from stamp fights. The reckless example loses to the champion's smash.
- **Orders size** under the orders (`ordersSize` in `src/view.js`): words and tokens, nothing else. It replaced the complexity meter on 2026-11-14 at the user's request. That meter's levels (instructions plus condition words; simple ≤ 7, moderate ≤ 11) rested on no evidence, and it miscounted real orders: the careful example has four rules but counted as 1 instruction and 2 conditions. With a model loaded, the worker counts tokens with that model's own tokenizer; WebLLM 0.2.88 has no public call for this, so it reads the loaded pipeline's tokenizer, and a failure falls back to the estimate. Before that, `words × 1.25 + 1`, shown as "about". On ten sample orders against the Qwen 2.5 tokenizer, that estimate was within 15% for plain sentences and up to 25% low for terse lists in capitals (`chars / 4` was off by up to 39%). Each turn's full prompt is about 220–370 tokens with the example orders; the context window is 2,048 tokens and the textarea stops at 1,800 characters, so the orders cannot overflow it.
- **Open idea, not built (user, 2026-11-14):** have the loaded model describe the orders itself. See the playtest questions.

Proxies (`node tools/balance.mjs`, 540 scripted order styles; before → after). The user's targets: at least 3 fight approaches win; reading clearly beats a fixed recipe; a good plan wins ≥ 70% with 10% random decisions; every stop and action is used by some winner; about half of simple orders win.

## Pause, stop after each turn, less repetition (2026-11-14, provisional until playtested)

User report: the information during play was confusing; Pause seemed not to pause; wanted a mode that stops after each hero action for review, with the model still working ahead. The previous flow is commit `5be3f6d`.

- **Pause freezes at once.** Before, Pause dropped the model's prepared reply but let the turn on screen play to its end (3–5 s, with sound), and greyed out Continue meanwhile; the status line even said "Game time is paused" during normal running. Now the beat clock stops (`gameTime` in `src/app.js`), the thinking phase holds, and a reply that arrives is held until Continue. Continue resumes from the same moment. A "Paused" tag sits on the stage's top edge; looping animations (smash pulse, thinking dots, weighing options, low-health flag) hold, but entrances finish so the frozen beat stays legible. Orders, assignment tabs and model settings unlock while paused; edited orders restart the trial on Continue.
- **Pause keeps prepared decisions.** They answer the exact states the run continues from, so nothing stale can act. Restart, a level, mode or model change, and a model error still drop them all.
- **Pace: Turn by turn** (the default, user request) replaced "One decision". A run plays one turn at normal speed, then stops with that turn's full play-by-play still on the stage and its options still stamped; the button reads "Next turn". It stops after forced moves too (they are rare: the start, and walking at full health). The status line says whether the next move is ready. It began as a separate "Stop after each turn" checkbox; the user asked for it as a pace instead ("Pause / Normal / Fast"). It is called "Turn by turn", not "Pause", because the Pause button already means "freeze now". The pace has a new storage key, so earlier saved paces (and the checkbox) do not carry over.
- **Options not taken** show as red, crossed-out pills with a ✕ in the play-by-play ("Guard instead of ✕ Drink potion ✕ Quick strike"), user request; before they were small grey struck-through words. The options panel strikes their titles in rust.
- **Read-ahead depth 3.** After each turn the model prepares up to three decisions ahead, each in the state the previous prepared decision leads to (a prepared decision is always the one used, so these are the states the hero will reach). Forced moves are stepped over. Prepared decisions are keyed by trial, state revision and the exact prompt. `QuestDemo.setReadAhead(n)` changes the depth for tests. Measured on the RTX 4090 circuit run: all 26 decisions ready before their options appeared (24 of 26 with the old one-ahead read), same choices as before.
- **One place per kind of information.** The turn card under the controls is gone: it repeated the stage's play-by-play while a turn played and the report after. The stage shows the turn being played (or kept at a stop); otherwise the situation for the next decision. The status line now says what the run is doing (running, paused, thinking, stopped) instead of "Decision made in 0.4 seconds". The counter reads "Turns used", and the options are numbered as the turn being decided ("Turn 7 · The hero weighs 5 options…"), so "Turn 6" no longer means two things at once.
- **Fixed on the way:** when the opponent acted on a turn's last beat, its box said "Now" over the *next* announced move (e.g. "Now: Smash, Charge" while the line said "Gore hit for 4"), because the last beat shows the state after the turn. Pause made this easy to see. Also, after a model error, a retry returned the same failed reply instead of asking again.
- In the Manual sandbox and in continuous runs, the previous turn is visible only while it plays and in the report. If that proves too little, the stop-each-turn mode is the intended answer, not a second copy of the turn.

## Random hero (added 2026-11-15, provisional until playtested)

User request: "add play option to let hero choose random action as alternative to follow prompt". The user chose to offer it on the published site as well as locally.

- **A third controller** under **Who plays the hero?**: Local LLM / Random / Manual sandbox. The published site shows two (the sandbox is `.manual-only`). The buttons sit in one row, one column per visible mode (`grid-auto-flow: column`), so the hidden sandbox takes no space. Locally, "Local LLM" wraps to two lines at 1440 px.
- **What it does** (`pickAtRandom` in `src/app.js`): at a real choice the options stay up for the usual 1.4 s ("The hero picks one of 3 at random…"), then one is picked uniformly with `Math.random` and played back like a model's choice, with the full 1.1 s reveal. Forced moves stay "No choice". No model is loaded or asked. The orders are not read, so the letter and the example orders are locked (disabled) while Random is selected, with a note in the letter saying why (user: "disable the "Write the orders" buttons/view when Random mode is selected"). The phone's "Orders in force" strip hides. The Manual sandbox only dims the letter; it was not asked for.
- **Same run controls** as the model hero: "Run at random", Pause (holds the pick), Continue, Turn by turn, Restart, the paces, Skip. A restart, level or mode change drops a pending pick by the same epoch check as model replies.
- **Labelled everywhere**: source `random` in the log and the export; "Random pick" in the report; "Picked at random" on the stage and the options. A trial with any random pick gets a verdict hint and a status line that credit luck ("Luck, not orders…"), since "The hero did exactly what the orders said" would be false.
- **Not a fallback.** Nothing switches to Random by itself, and model errors do not suggest it.
- **Unseeded**, so a random run cannot be replayed exactly; the log records every pick.
- **Baseline** (100,000 simulated trials each, uniform picks among the offered actions; scratch script, not in the repo):

  | | First instructions | Fair warning | Prize money | Full circuit |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Won | 67% | 2.0% | 3.4% | 0.2% |
  | Died | 0% | 0.6% | 13% | 16% |
  | Out of turns | 33% | 97% | 84% | 84% |

  On the first assignment, random play beats the unfortunate first draft ("Always retreat…"), which always loses. Most random losses after that are the clock, not death.

## Temperature (added 2026-11-15, provisional until playtested)

User question: "can we set temperature in game?", after the Random baseline.

- **A slider** under **Change model or check this device**: 0 to 1.5 in steps of 0.1, default 0, stored per browser (`pmlq.temperature`). Above 0 it also shows in the model line ("· temperature 0.7"). It locks while a run moves, like the model select.
- **Fixed per trial**, like the orders: `runTemperature` is set when a trial starts, and a change mid-trial applies from the next trial (the note under the slider says so). Prepared decisions are keyed by it, so read-ahead never mixes two temperatures in one trial. The export records `temperature`, and each decision returns the value it was made at.
- **Both backends.** WebLLM gets it as `temperature`. The CPU model samples (`do_sample`) among the offered JSON strings only. The worker clamps it to 0–2.
- **Every recorded model result in these notes is at 0**, except the 0.7 runs of 2026-11-24 ("Example orders, the prize fight, and what sways the 3B": two rehearsals of 840 decisions, 3–4% of answers different from 0, and whole trials of the example orders). Above 0 a run does not repeat, so one run says little.
- **Measured** (Qwen 2.5 3B, careful circuit orders, 20 asks per temperature): at the boar's first charge, where the orders say to guard, the model chose quick strike 20 of 20 times at 0, guard 6 of 20 at 0.7, and guard 12 of 20 at 1.5. On the road it advanced 20 of 20 at every temperature. So temperature can also help: there the greedy choice is the wrong one.
- Tools: `QUEST_TEMPERATURE` for `npm run test:llm`, `PROBE_TEMPERATURE` for `tools/probe.mjs`. The trial records include the temperature.
- Not checked: the CPU model above 0.

## Parallel model runs (measured 2026-11-15)

Question: can trials run in parallel, to make 1000+ model runs practical? Scratch benchmark, not in the repo: the real worker, Qwen 2.5 3B, 31 circuit decision states with the careful orders, temperature 0, 30 decisions per model copy, RTX 4090 with nothing else running.

| Model copies | In one browser | In separate browsers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.90 decisions/s (522 ms each) | – |
| 2 | 2.22 (895 ms) | 2.19 (907 ms) |
| 3 | 2.29 (1296 ms) | 2.28 (1310 ms) |
| 4 | 2.30 (1735 ms) | the 4th browser got no WebGPU adapter |

- Extra copies add at most about 20%. The GPU reports 100% busy at about 230 W of its 450 W: the copies take turns on the GPU instead of running together, whether they share a browser or not. Each copy uses about 2.1 GB.
- Where one decision's time goes (WebLLM's usage report, tutorial, warm): 0.52 s reading the ~260-token prompt (about 500 tokens/s) and 0.15 s writing ~7 tokens. Reading the prompt is about 80%.
- At 1.9–2.3 decisions/s, 1000 full-circuit trials (~26 decisions each) take about 3.2–3.8 hours, and 1000 Fair warning trials (~15) about 2 hours, with playback off. At temperature 0, repeating the same orders only repeats the same trial.
- Untested ways to go faster: keep the unchanging start of the prompt (system prompt and orders) cached between decisions instead of `resetChat` each time; or a native runtime with batching, whose results would need checking against the browser model's.

## Determinism

The engine is deterministic given a trial's seed: fixed course, and in Fair warning and Prize money the opponents' move order is drawn from the seed (`newGame(level, seed)`; see "Drawn move order"). Without a seed every routine is fixed, which is what the tests, the tools, the read-back drill and the experiments use. The app draws a seed per trial with `Math.random`; the saved report includes the state, and so the seed. The music rolls its own dice (random patterns and notes) and never reads or changes game state. Model output is not guaranteed to repeat across devices or model builds, and above temperature 0 it does not repeat at all.
